A monthly profit and loss statement is not a courtesy document — it is the primary diagnostic instrument for your trucking LLC's financial health. [IRS Publication 583](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p583) establishes the foundational recordkeeping obligation: every business must maintain books sufficient to determine correct tax liability, and the P&L is the organizing structure through which that determination flows. Owner-operators who treat the P&L as an annual tax-season exercise rather than a monthly operational tool routinely underestimate fixed cost creep, miscategorize deductible expenses, and accept loads that destroy margin.

## Why the Monthly Profit and Loss Statement Trucking Operations Require Is Different from a Generic P&L

Standard small-business P&L templates collapse categories that trucking demands you separate. A trucking P&L must distinguish fuel — a variable, load-linked cost — from fixed overhead such as base insurance premiums and truck payments. It must isolate per-mile costs from time-based costs. This structural specificity is not aesthetic; it directly determines which deductions survive IRS scrutiny and which load decisions are rational.

### The Recordkeeping Standard Under IRS Publication 583

Publication 583 requires that your records clearly show gross income, deductions, and credits. For a trucking LLC, that means every revenue entry must be traceable to a rate confirmation or bill of lading, and every deduction must be supported by a contemporaneous receipt or mileage log. The IRS does not specify a single bookkeeping format, but it does require that records be available for examination — generally for a minimum of three years from the date the return was filed, or two years from the date tax was paid, whichever is later. Commingled personal and business accounts make this audit trail functionally impossible; the structural reasons are detailed in this post on [separating business and personal finances](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/separating-business-and-personal-finances-why-commingling-si/).

## Constructing the Revenue Section

Gross revenue in trucking is not simply the linehaul rate. Fuel surcharges, detention pay, layover compensation, and accessorial fees are all gross income and must appear above the line. Record each load separately by settlement date, not by delivery date, to match your broker or carrier settlement cycle and maintain cash-basis accuracy. If your LLC operates under its own authority, your freight invoices are the source document; if you lease to a carrier, the settlement statement serves that function.

Load-level revenue tracking feeds directly into the load acceptance decision framework covered in this analysis of [load-by-load profitability](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/load-by-load-profitability-should-you-take-this-load-or-dead/). A monthly P&L that lacks load-level granularity in the revenue section cannot tell you which lanes are profitable and which are subsidizing the business.

## Structuring the Expense Section

### Fixed vs. Variable Cost Classification

Fixed costs — truck payment, base liability insurance, physical damage insurance, occupational accident coverage, and any IFTA/IRP registration fees — appear every month regardless of miles driven. Variable costs — fuel, driver pay if applicable, tolls, scales, and maintenance — are load and mile dependent. This bifurcation is not optional: it is the only structure that produces a meaningful break-even analysis.

Your fully-loaded cost per mile, calculated using the method outlined in this [cost-per-mile formula post](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/cost-per-mile-calculation-the-formula-every-owner-operator-n/), is the variable by which each revenue line on your P&L is evaluated. As a worked example: if your fixed monthly overhead is $4,200 and your variable cost runs $1.45 per mile, a month with 8,000 revenue miles requires at least $15,800 in gross revenue to break even — meaning your blended loaded rate must clear $1.975 per mile before a dollar of net income is recorded.

The four expense categories that most frequently produce IRS mismatches in trucking audits are:

- **Fuel:** Must be supported by receipts linking quantity, location, and date — critical for IFTA reconciliation covered in the [IRP and IFTA registration guide](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/irp-and-ifta-registration-and-fuel-tax-requirements-for-inte/)
- **Depreciation:** Elected under IRC §179 or MACRS; the deduction method must be consistent year-over-year and documented in your depreciation schedule
- **Meals:** Subject to the applicable IRS percentage limitation for business travel away from home; the full meal cost is not deductible
- **Insurance proceeds and accident-related costs:** Settlement payments received are gross income; repair expenses and legal fees are deductible — documentation requirements are substantial, as outlined in this [post-accident paper trail guide](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/the-paper-trail-after-an-accident-every-document-you-need-an/)

## Net Income, Self-Employment Tax Exposure, and the S-Corp Election

Net income on a single-member LLC P&L flows to Schedule C, where it is subject to self-employment tax on the full net amount. At meaningful income levels, this exposure materially exceeds the entity-level tax planning available through an S-corporation election, which allows the owner to take a reasonable salary (subject to FICA) and treat residual profit as a distribution not subject to self-employment tax. The [IRS Small Business and Self-Employed Tax Center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) provides the operative guidance on entity classification and estimated tax obligations. The P&L is the instrument that makes this planning decision quantifiable — without clean monthly statements, you cannot determine whether the administrative cost of S-corp payroll is justified by the SE tax savings.

## Closing the Month: Reconciliation Before Filing

A P&L is only as accurate as the bank reconciliation behind it. Before closing any month, reconcile every line item against your business checking and fuel card statements. Unreconciled months compound — a $300 uncategorized charge in March becomes a $3,600 annualized distortion in your Schedule C deductions. Close the month within the first ten business days of the following month while source documents are current.
