Most owner-operators open a bank account, start hauling, and figure the books will sort themselves out by tax season. They don't. A poorly structured general ledger collapses load-level profitability into a single undifferentiated revenue line, making it impossible to isolate which lanes are profitable and which are subsidizing your worst customers. The fix is structural, not behavioral — and it starts with a trucking chart of accounts designed around the cost architecture of a one-truck operation before the first invoice is issued.

## Why the Trucking Chart of Accounts Is a Tax Document First

[IRS Publication 583](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p583) is unambiguous: you must keep records that substantiate income, deductions, and credits claimed on your return. The chart of accounts is the skeleton those records hang on. If your account structure doesn't map cleanly to Schedule C line items or, for a multi-member entity, Form 1065, you will spend reconciliation hours reconstructing categories that a proper COA would have captured automatically.

Before you build the ledger, make sure the legal entity is correctly formed. Issues explored in [Forming a Trucking LLC: State Selection, Registered Agent, and Filing Requirements](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/forming-a-trucking-llc-state-selection-registered-agent-and/) — including registered agent obligations and state-specific formation costs — directly determine how your chart of accounts handles member equity and owner draws versus guaranteed payments.

### Revenue Accounts: Freight, Fuel Surcharge, and Accessorials Are Not the Same Account

Collapsing all revenue into one "Freight Income" account destroys your ability to analyze rate-per-mile by lane or evaluate whether fuel surcharge recovery actually offsets your fuel spend. At minimum, a one-truck COA should carry discrete revenue accounts for: base freight revenue, fuel surcharge income, detention and layover fees, and any TONU (truck order not used) recoveries. This separation also simplifies broker reconciliation disputes and supports the paper trail requirements discussed in [The Paper Trail After an Accident: Every Document You Need](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/the-paper-trail-after-an-accident-every-document-you-need-an/) — where commingled revenue records create evidentiary problems that extend well beyond insurance claims.

## Building the Expense Side of the Trucking Chart of Accounts

The expense architecture for a one-truck operation should mirror the variable/fixed cost distinction that drives real profitability decisions. Variable costs scale with miles driven; fixed costs accrue whether the truck moves or not. A COA that conflates the two obscures your true break-even and prevents you from correctly evaluating marginal loads.

### Variable Operating Costs

These accounts should map directly to per-mile cost tracking:

- **Fuel** — separate from fuel surcharge income; track net fuel cost after surcharge recovery
- **Driver wages or owner draws** — if you add a W-2 driver, this becomes a payroll expense with FICA implications
- **Factoring fees** — deductible as a financial service expense; often misbooked as a revenue reduction
- **Load-specific permits and tolls** — distinguish from annual licensing; these are variable per-dispatch costs
- **Lumper and load-assist fees** — frequently expensed informally without a corresponding account, creating audit exposure

*Numeric example:* On a 1,200-mile load grossing $2,400, if fuel costs $0.58/mile, tolls total $34, and a factoring fee runs 3% ($72), your variable cost is $830 — producing a variable contribution of $1,570 before fixed-cost allocation. That calculation requires three distinct COA accounts to produce automatically.

### Fixed and Semi-Fixed Costs

Truck payments or depreciation, insurance premiums, base licensing and registration, and accounting/professional fees belong in fixed-cost accounts. Depreciation warrants its own sub-account because it affects your tax basis and interacts with Section 179 elections — a point the [IRS Small Business and Self-Employed Tax Center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) addresses in its depreciation and basis resources. Conflating depreciation with "truck expense" is one of the most common bookkeeping errors in small carrier operations.

### Entity-Level Compliance Costs

If your LLC is subject to BOI filing requirements under the Corporate Transparency Act, the associated professional fees belong in a discrete compliance expense account — not miscellaneous. The filing obligations and penalties analyzed in [BOI Reporting and the Corporate Transparency Act: What Trucking LLCs Need to Know](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/boi-reporting-and-the-corporate-transparency-act-what-trucki/) represent a real ongoing cost category that should be visible in your ledger, not buried.

## Equity, Owner Draws, and the Single-Member Trap

For a single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity, there are no salary accounts — owner compensation flows through the equity draw account. Booking draws as an expense inflates your apparent operating costs, distorts your net income for Schedule SE purposes, and misrepresents the business's profitability to any lender reviewing financials. Your COA needs a clean equity section: member capital contributions, member draws, and retained earnings as separate accounts.

This structure also matters operationally. If you ever hire a driver, the entity's reporting posture shifts materially — a dynamic covered in [Driver Turnover and Compliance Risk: Why High-Turnover Fleets Pay More](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/driver-turnover-and-compliance-risk-why-high-turnover-fleets/), which quantifies the financial exposure of misclassifying or poorly onboarding employed drivers.

Understanding your own authority and how it shapes your revenue relationships is equally foundational — the common mistakes outlined in [What Owner-Operators Get Wrong About Their Own Operating Authority](https://blog.thetruckercodex.com/what-owner-operators-get-wrong-about-their-own-operating-aut/) often trace back to COA errors that blur the line between brokered and direct revenue.

## Implementation Standard

Publication 583 does not mandate a specific account numbering system, but standard practice assigns 1000-series to assets, 2000-series to liabilities, 3000-series to equity, 4000-series to revenue, and 5000–6000-series to expenses. Set this up correctly in your accounting software before you record a single transaction — retrofitting a COA mid-year is technically possible but operationally disruptive and introduces reclassification errors.
